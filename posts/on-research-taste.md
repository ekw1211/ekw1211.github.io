---
title: On Developing Research Taste
date: 2024-11-12
description: Some thoughts on how to find problems worth working on.
---

Early in graduate school, I was told that the hardest skill to develop is not technical — it's the ability to recognize a good problem when you see one.

Technique can be learned from textbooks and papers. Problem selection is something else. It requires a kind of aesthetic judgment that doesn't transfer easily from supervisor to student, and that takes years of immersion to develop.

## What makes a problem good?

A good research problem is, at minimum, *open* and *important*. But importance is the harder criterion to assess. Importance to whom? On what timescale? With what assumptions about the field's future direction?

I've found it useful to think in terms of leverage: does solving this problem unlock other problems? A result that opens ten new directions is worth more than one that closes a narrow question.

## The role of taste

Taste, in research as in art, is the accumulated residue of attention. You develop it by reading widely, by noticing what makes certain papers feel inevitable in retrospect, and by practicing the slow work of asking *why this, why now*.

No shortcuts. But the process is its own reward.
